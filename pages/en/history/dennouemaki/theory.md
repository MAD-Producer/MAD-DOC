# Classification, Views on Creativity, and Copyright

This page summarizes the five more theoretical, opinion-based “Column” articles: two on classification, a short essay on the nature of MAD, a note on copyright, and an essay on generational change in the community. These mainly reflect the author’s personal position and are all rated **C Personal opinion**.

> See the [Column Summaries overview](./) for the source registration and evidence ratings. MAD DOC’s own framework is described in [Classification](/en/mad/classification).

## The Third MAD: The “New Video” Sphere {#third-mad}

Source: [第三のMAD](https://dennouemaki.blog.jp/2018/11/11/%E7%AC%AC%E4%B8%89%E3%81%AEmad/) (2018-11-11) · Evidence **C**

### Five Cultural Spheres in Two Branches

The author argues that MAD movies fall into five cultural spheres according to their content and concept, and into two branches according to the nature of the media:

| Branch | Cultural sphere | Starting point given by the author |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dynamic content | 1. Audio | From 1982, *New Mad Tape* |
| Dynamic content | 2. Video | From 1984, *Etoketto Video* |
| Dynamic content | 3. Derivative creation | Developed after *DAICON IV* (1981) |
| Static content | 4. Still image | Around 1997 |
| Static content | 5. Flash | 2002, founding of the Flash board on 2ch; the author notes Flash support ended in 2020 |

**Editorial note**: The year given here for *New Mad Tape* (1982) conflicts with the 1978 date the same author adopts in the [etymology article](./origins#etymology). The original offers no explanation, and the date is reproduced as written.

### A Sixth Category: Mixed Content

- The author holds that a new kind of video has appeared in recent years that is neither dynamic nor static and belongs to none of the spheres above. He provisionally calls it the “**new video**” sphere (新動画系), starting from *Secret Waltz* (2003).
- Definition: mixed-content, music-video-style MAD that draws on video, still-image, and hand-drawn material in combination. The author admits it should strictly be called composite, fused, mixed MAD, but uses “new video” because that is too long.
- Characteristics: still-image techniques applied to video footage, anime footage blended with CG imagery, heavy use of kinetic typography and motion effects, and highly advanced editing; some works also use hand-drawn or 3D elements.
- The author recalls that when **軍魔** (Gunma) and **Piano** released *Secret Waltz* in 2003, he was stunned and unsure how to judge it, a frustration that lingered for years. He finally concluded that a work with different content and concept cannot be measured by the yardstick of video MAD and should be treated as a separate cultural sphere.
- Fifteen years after *Secret Waltz*, in 2018, the author notes that this has become one of the most common genres.

## Classifying MAD {#classification}

Source: [MADの分類](https://dennouemaki.blog.jp/2018/11/11/mad%E3%81%AE%E5%88%86%E9%A1%9E/) (2018-11-11) · Evidence **C**

The author proposes a three-layer classification: by cultural sphere, by genre, and by editing technique.

### 1. By Cultural Sphere (6 Categories)

| Cultural sphere | Description |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Audio | Sound-centered works such as MAD tapes and otoMAD |
| 2. Video | Works using animation or live-action footage |
| 3. Still image | Works using games, manga, and other static material |
| 4. Derivative creation | Hand-drawn MAD and similar works |
| 5. Flash | Creative MAD derived from 2ch memes and the like (Flash support ended in 2020) |
| 6. New video | Works combining the content of categories 1–4; see [the previous section](#third-mad) |

### 2. By Genre (2 Branches, 7 Categories)

| Branch | Categories |
| --- | --- |
| Music | 1) Music video　2) Opening movie |
| Variety | 3) Comedy　4) Sketch (*konto*)　5) Nonfiction　6) Supplementary footage　7) Chaos |

The author’s notes:

- For the difference between comedy and sketch, he cites the common saying that comedy is performed by actors and sketches by comedians.
- Chaos is a new genre that emerged in the 2000s; the other six, in the author’s view, were established by Jūnishidan in 1984. (On the year, see the [comparison table on the origins page](./origins#comparing-the-two-accounts).)

### Comparison with MAD DOC’s Classification

- MAD DOC’s [five-dimensional classification](/en/mad/classification) works along five axes: source media, expressive purpose, production techniques, collaboration structure, and release context. This article instead combines “cultural sphere, genre, technique” into a single tree.
- The author’s “cultural sphere” roughly corresponds to MAD DOC’s “source media” axis and “genre” to “expressive purpose,” while “editing technique” mixes technique with subject matter.
- The author’s scheme grows out of the Japanese videotape-to-Niconico scene and should not be taken as a globally applicable definition.

## Is MAD Derivative Work, Original Work, or Creative Activity? {#is-mad-creative}

Source: [MADは二次か・創作か・クリエイティブか](https://dennouemaki.blog.jp/2018/11/22/mad%E3%81%AF%E4%BA%8C%E6%AC%A1%E3%81%8B%E3%83%BB%E5%89%B5%E4%BD%9C%E3%81%8B%E3%83%BB%E3%82%AF%E3%83%AA%E3%82%A8%E3%82%A4%E3%83%86%E3%82%A3%E3%83%96%E3%81%8B/) (2018-11-22) · Evidence **C**

The author says he has written and deleted this piece many times over several years, knowing the topic is explosive, but still wants readers to understand it correctly.

### Three Definitions

He begins with the definitions he uses:

- **Original creation**: something made for the first time.
- **Derivative creation (*niji sōsaku*)**: a work that does not infringe the rights of reproduction or integrity and retains the essential features of the original.
- **Creative**: coming up with novel, original, and productive ideas, or the ability to do so.

### The Author’s Three Conclusions

1. **MAD is not derivative creation**: The author considers the term “derivative creation” itself untenable, though he deliberately does not say where the problem lies. He takes the fact that many wikis call MAD derivative work as a kindly meant description.
2. **Nor is MAD an original creative work**: His reasoning is that if it does not even qualify as derivative, it cannot meet the definition of an original work. He separately notes that the genres, techniques, and methods of MAD-making are ideas and do not themselves qualify as copyrighted works.
3. **MAD’s creativity holds only within limits**: Looking only at individual techniques and ideas, creativity and productivity can be demonstrated. But producing a different meaning from a specific copyrighted work touches on the right of integrity, so he advises against promoting this loudly.

He sums up his position as follows: MAD, which can never become anything in its own right, is a “**secondary creation of interpretation**.”

### The Author’s Own Experience

- Long troubled by this question, the author made 「マイケルジャクソン MAD」 (*Michael Jackson MAD*) around 2002; in its upload title he calls it the “original complex MAD.”
- At the time he called his peers “MAD creators,” but he meant it as a metaphor: MAD is not *evil*, but MAD makers really are *bad boys* (ワル).
- The response was unexpectedly enthusiastic, and he missed the chance to explain what he meant, so he uses this article to set it down.

## MAD and Copyright Law: What Changes Without the Complaint Requirement? {#copyright}

Source: [MADと著作権法、非親告罪でどう変わるか？](https://dennouemaki.blog.jp/2018/12/15/mad%E3%81%A8%E8%91%97%E4%BD%9C%E6%A8%A9%E6%B3%95/) (2018-12-15) · Evidence **C** (described by the author as a personal memo; legal information reflects the situation in 2018 and is not legal advice)

### Background Concepts

The author first explains several concepts in plain terms:

- **Civil law**: rules for settling disputes.
- **Criminal law**: rules for deciding right and wrong when a dispute cannot be settled.
- **Copyright**: in the author’s view, a form of intellectual property under civil law.
- **Complaint offense (*shinkokuzai*)**: a crime that cannot be prosecuted without a complaint from the injured party.
- **Non-complaint offense**: removing that complaint requirement.

In the author’s view, the “rights” in intellectual property are legal tools that protect rights holders who suffer losses when others use their work without permission, covering the creator’s rightful income, licensing, and the ability to assert the right against third parties. Copyright is often discussed as a law that punishes, but he stresses that it is first of all a civil rule for mediating disputes.

### What Removing the Complaint Requirement Means

- Previously, only the injured rights holder could bring a case. Without the complaint requirement, **anyone can report an infringement**; the author notes that even reporting someone out of personal dislike becomes possible.
- Reports cannot be made arbitrarily, however, because the copyright law specifies the purposes and acts that constitute a violation. The requirements he quotes amount to:
  - **Purpose**: obtaining financial gain in return, or harming the profit the rights holder would expect from providing a paid work;
  - **Act**: transferring to the public, or transmitting to the public, unaltered copies of a paid work, or copying it for that purpose;
  - **Limitation**: only where, in light of the type and use of the work, the number and manner of copies, and other circumstances, the rights holder’s expected profit is **unjustly harmed**.

### The Japanese Industry’s Past Stance

The author summarizes why Japan, and the otaku industry in particular, tolerated dōjin works and MAD:

- They raise a title’s profile;
- Litigation is expensive;
- The sums are small but the paperwork is enormous;
- They effectively train the next generation of otaku at no cost to the industry.

He contrasts this with a Western culture in which, as he puts it, suing is what counts. He describes the Diet deliberations on the TPP-related bills as the source of outside pressure, and relays news reports that Prime Minister Shinzō Abe stated in Diet testimony that derivative works and dōjinshi would be excluded from non-complaint treatment.

### The Author’s Personal View

- Whether something is legal or not is for the law to decide, not for creators.
- He distinguishes **conflict ≠ violation ≠ crime**: in his view, MAD conflicts with the law, but unless the law hands down a judgment, it cannot be called a crime. He declines to say more.
- He poses a thought experiment: if MAD promoted a work and the rights holder’s income clearly rose, a kind of reverse comparative negligence, could MAD makers claim a share? He concedes that this is unrealistic and that anyone who tried would likely be sued by the public instead.
- He urges readers to start by understanding the facts correctly, and closes by telling MAD editors to hold their heads high as the unseen editors who support the otaku industry.

### Postscript: Two Possible Futures

- The author admits he wrote the article after drinking and adds a postscript: if Western-style non-complaint offenses were adopted, it is obvious whether Japan’s Niconico or the West’s YouTube would come out ahead.
- His alternative scenario: the culture of MAD screening events might return to the spotlight.

### References in the Original

The author lists: the Japanese Wikipedia talk page on non-complaint offenses in Japanese copyright law; an explainer on whether derivative work is a crime if the rights holder does not sue; a news report on Prime Minister Abe’s Diet answer; Tarō Yamada’s series on expression regulation and non-complaint offenses; the TPP-related legal amendment act; and a report on an Agency for Cultural Affairs committee discussing the exclusion of dōjinshi from non-complaint treatment.

## A “Newtype” Theory of MAD {#newtype}

Source: [MADにおけるニュータイプ論](https://dennouemaki.blog.jp/2020/07/25/mad%E3%81%AB%E3%81%8A%E3%81%91%E3%82%8B%E3%83%8B%E3%83%A5%E3%83%BC%E3%82%BF%E3%82%A4%E3%83%97%E8%AB%96/) (2020-07-25) · Evidence **C** (personal essay with community recollections)

### 1. What Is a Newtype?

- The author often compares today’s young MAD editors to “Newtypes,” the humans with extraordinary powers in *Mobile Suit Gundam*.
- He explains briefly that there are several definitions; roughly, a Newtype has a kind of evolved telepathy. *Gundam NT* depicts Newtypes as people who understand one another and need not fight, yet war never actually disappeared, which he takes to mean that human nature does not change.

### 2. MAD as a Set of Foreign Cultures

- Although all called MAD, the field subdivides into video, still image, multi-source, hand-drawn, live-action recreation, NicoM@s, otoMAD, and more.
- Publishing channels include Niconico, YouTube, TikTok, bilibili, and MAD screening events, as well as sharing high-quality versions via cloud storage; some AMV communities also run their own servers.
- He concludes that MAD video, which began with a single tape, has over 36 years (as of 2020) branched into many communities, genres, and cultures.

### 3. MAD’s Underground Era

- Before 2000, when the internet was not yet widespread, community life centered on meeting in person at dōjin events: chatting while watching MAD, wandering Akihabara, drinking together, and debating famous MAD videos.
- After 2ch appeared, the community moved online. The author believes anonymous posting eroded manners and pushed MAD into an underground era.
- He recalls that the scene already drew people with strong opinions and egos, and with copyright’s gray areas on top, there was a constant storm of criticism, demands, condemnation, and attacks on both quality and character. Many people quit. He writes that the universal age of the internet turned hopeful prayers for the future into curses.
- He says that he rarely appeared in public and so was not attacked himself, but grew tired of the atmosphere and stepped away from MAD editing for a while. He also admits that, being severely socially awkward, he found offline screenings burdensome: constant requests, participation contracts, teasing of creators, in-jokes when he missed a deadline, and the same old stories retold at drinking parties every year.

### 4. A New Era for MAD

- More than a decade later, the author happened to browse Niconico Douga and found many MAD events under way: participants praising each other’s strengths, raising each other up, and very young creators producing work at an astonishing technical level, all apparently having a great time. He says it made him itch to create again.
- To him, this was exactly the theme set by Jūnishidan, the founders of MAD: an event that attendees and participants alike can enjoy.
- He later learned that quite a few users had worked quietly behind the scenes to improve the community’s culture.

### 5. The First Newtype?

- The first creator who struck the author as a new kind of human was **Piano** (around 2004?), whom he describes as the first to bring AMV techniques into MAD and attempt multi-source MAD.
- When he later discovered **しょー** (Shō), he was delighted to see what looked like an evolved form of Piano’s approach.

### 6. What Matters in Cross-Cultural Exchange

- Individual expression, opinions, cultures, and languages exist in as many forms as there are creators.
- His lesson from studying MAD’s different cultures: “You don’t have to accept another culture; you only have to understand it.”

### 7. The Gundam MAD in Progress

- The author reveals that he is making a MAD using *Mobile Suit Gundam* for AniPAFE 2020. His motive is a hope that communication beyond language will grow and that MAD culture will leap beyond Japan to become a subculture Japan can be proud of.
- He calls on “Oldtypes” like himself (self-deprecatingly, *rōgai*, “old nuisances”) to guide and watch over the coming era.
