# Origins, Etymology, and Jūnishidan

::: tip Key points
- **What “MAD” means**: according to tencho, it comes from the liner notes of *NEW MAD TAPE* (1978): after hearing it, the songs in your head are scrambled and you cannot sing them the original way.
- **The earliest audio MAD**: “奇知害低符,” made in 1976 by students at Osaka University of Arts.
- **The first MAD video**: the 1985 “Jūnishidan Video” by Kindai University's Jūnishidan, then called a “parody edit video.”
- **Two accounts**: in 2018 the author named the 1984 “Etoketto Video”; after the 2020 interview he revised it to the account above. This page keeps both.
- **Jūnishidan's motto**: the really fun things are the ones you make yourself.
:::

This page summarizes four articles from the “Genealogy of MAD” subcategory of the Dennou Emaki blog. *The Genealogy of MAD* (2018) is the author’s first timeline. In April 2020, after interviewing **tencho**, leader of the group Jūnishidan, the author published three articles that revise and expand it. The two accounts differ, so this page summarizes them in order of publication and ends with a comparison table.

> See the [Column Summaries overview](/en/history/dennouemaki) for the source registration and how reliability is labeled.

## The 2018 Genealogy: From MAD Tape to AI Editing {#lineage-2018}

Source: [MADの系譜](https://dennouemaki.blog.jp/2018/11/10/mad%E3%81%AE%E7%B3%BB%E8%AD%9C/) (2018-11-10) · Reliability: **Community record / Personal view**

The author divides MAD history into seven milestones, each marking a shift in technology or distribution.

### 1976: The Origin of MAD in Tape Collage

- The starting point is an audio-collage tape titled 「奇知害低符」 (*Kichigai Teifu*), also called the “MAD tape.” It was made at Osaka University of Arts by **MAD島川** (MAD Shimakawa) and **ネトホヘル矢野** (Netohoheru Yano), the manga artist **Kentarō Yano**.
- **クレイジィ今井** (Crazy Imai) later remade and re-edited it, and it spread under the title *NEW MAD TAPE*.
- Kentarō Yano founded “Group CAS,” the manga and animation research club at Osaka University of Arts. Other records credit MAD Shimakawa as the originator of the first MAD TAPE.
- The author notes that the story is well known because Kazuhiko Shimamoto’s manga *Aoi Honoo* (*Blue Blazes*) depicts it.

### 1981: The Arrival of Independent Animation

- The opening animations of DAICON III and DAICON IV, produced by DAICON FILM, strongly influenced later MAD videos and derivative content.
- The author stresses that these are independent animations with a high degree of originality. They are often confused with MAD but should be kept distinct.
- In image captions, the author jokingly remarks on the copyright status of the characters and imagery used in these works.

### 1984: The Birth of the MAD Video

- At this point the author held that the *Etoketto Video* series, collage parody videos by Jūnishidan, were the first MAD videos.
- The author gives two reasons: no record of any MAD video exists before 1984, and leading figures in the MAD scene agree.
- The author also speculates that because the series appeared in Osaka at the same time as DAICON FILM, it may have been influenced by it.
- The author emphasizes that this series single-handedly laid the foundation for almost every MAD genre that exists today, which is why it is called the MAD video that is “both origin and summit.”

> The author revised the year and title in this section after the 2020 interview; see [The Revised Origin Timeline](#origin-1973) below.

### 1998: The Start of the Nonlinear Editing Era

- Around 1996–97, some creators began nonlinear editing on the Mac.
- Windows 98 came out in 1998, along with many video capture cards affordable to casual users. The number of MAD editors rose sharply as a result.
- Peer-to-peer file sharing made MAD explosively better known in the same period.

### 2007: Niconico Douga Launches

- Convenient streaming and easy sharing gave rise to new communities. Production moved entirely to digital editing, making it easier for younger people to start.
- In the author’s view, however, the culture of the “MAD video” (a compilation on tape) faded, and the term shifted to “MAD movie,” meaning a single work.
- Activity split into two spheres: online users on Niconico and YouTube, and offline users at underground screening events and similar venues.

### 2018: The Mobile Editing Era

- The very notion of “capture” is long gone; editing has moved to mobile devices.
- Ownership of personal computers is declining, while mobile environments are advancing rapidly.
- Creators keep getting younger, and some junior high and high school students have begun making MAD.

### 202?: An AI Editing Era?

- The author half-jokingly predicts that AI editing will free people from the agony of deadlines, that social media will shift to VR, and that VR screening events will begin.
- He links a 2018 roundup of AI video editing services, noting that some of this already exists.

The article ends with a genealogy chart, “MADの系譜.pdf” (originally hosted on dennouemaki.info; it no longer opens).

## The Etymology of MAD (1978) {#etymology}

Source: [MADの語源（1978年）](https://dennouemaki.blog.jp/2020/04/25/mad%E3%81%AE%E8%AA%9E%E6%BA%90%EF%BC%881978%E5%B9%B4%EF%BC%89/) (2020-04-25) · Reliability: **Community record** (information provided by tencho of Jūnishidan)

### What “MAD” Means

- The author had earlier written on the blog that MAD meant “the creator is mad.” tencho of Jūnishidan happened to read this and corrected him.
- According to tencho, the word comes from the liner notes of *NEW MAD TAPE* (1978). In paraphrase, the notes said that, back when dōjinshi conventions were not yet common, a suspicious tape circulated south of Lake Biwa, and anyone who heard it could no longer sing songs properly.
- MAD therefore originally meant that **the listener’s memory goes mad**: when singing an anime song, one cannot help singing the edited version.
- The same applies to MAD videos: hearing an anime’s opening theme brings to mind only the other, edited footage, and this phenomenon was likewise called MAD.
- The author suggests that “south of Lake Biwa” refers to the Osaka area, where Osaka University of Arts is located, or more broadly to the Kinki region, where dōjinshi conventions were active.

### Where “MAD VIDEO” Came From

- Around 1985, MAD tapes were well known in dōjin circles, but edited video was still in a transitional phase, somewhere between independent production, parody video, and opening-sequence compilation.
- Creators rarely met one another, so there was no common name. Such works were called “parody video,” “edited video,” or “edited parody video.”
- Later, a creator called Studio Maruki released a video titled *MAD VTR*, which tencho estimates was around 1990. The term “MAD video” may have spread from there.
- The index image of *NEW MAD TAPE* in the article was provided by tencho.

## The Revised Origin Timeline (1973–1985) {#origin-1973}

Source: [MADの起源（1973年）](https://dennouemaki.blog.jp/2020/04/25/mad%E3%81%AE%E8%B5%B7%E6%BA%90%EF%BC%881973%E5%B9%B4%EF%BC%89/) (2020-04-25) · Reliability: **Community record** (tencho’s recollection)

After interviewing tencho, the author reorganized the timeline as follows:

| Year | Milestone | Work / event |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1973 | The origin of MAD | Radio show *Kin-chan no Don to Itte Miyou!* |
| 1976 | Audio MAD appears | *Kichigai Teifu* |
| 1980 | tencho experiments with parody videos as a student | — |
| 1984 | A transitional period for parody | *Bisuketto Video* |
| 1985 | The MAD video appears | *Jūnishidan Video* |

### 1973: *Kin-chan no Don to Itte Miyou!*

- tencho believes MAD can be traced back to the “Record Daisakusen” (“Operation Record”) segment of this radio show, which began around 1973.
- In the segment, records were cut and spliced based on listeners’ ideas, or song lyrics were made to banter with the host, Kin’ichi Hagimoto. The author considers this a MAD tape in all but name.
- Several books collecting listener submissions were also published. tencho says the segment inspired him to make various experiments on cassette at the time.

### 1976: *Kichigai Teifu*

- In 1976, *Kichigai Teifu*, made by MAD Shimakawa and Netohoheru Yano (Kentarō Yano) of Osaka University of Arts, is regarded as the oldest audio MAD.
- Note that the 2018 version calls this year “the origin of MAD,” whereas the 2020 version presents it as “the appearance of audio MAD” and moves the origin back to 1973.

### 1984: *Bisuketto Video*

- The author calls this work a “Pandora’s box” that he prefers not to discuss publicly, hinting only that it was mentioned on a Niconico livestream.
- For details of its content, see [Jūnishidan’s Predecessor](#bisuketto) below.

### 1985: *Jūnishidan Video*

- In 1985, the *Jūnishidan Video* made by Kikaku Group Jūnishidan of Kindai University is regarded as the **first MAD video recognized as such by the public**.
- At the time it was called an “edited parody video”; only later did the name “MAD video” take hold.
- The author concludes that volunteers in the Kansai region, centered on Osaka University of Arts and Kindai University, were the ones who spread this parody culture.

## Kikaku Group Jūnishidan {#junishidan}

Source: [MAD動画の創始者「企画グループ十二支団」（1985年）](https://dennouemaki.blog.jp/2020/04/25/mad%E5%8B%95%E7%94%BB%E3%81%AE%E5%89%B5%E5%A7%8B%E8%80%85%E3%80%8C%E4%BC%81%E7%94%BB%E3%82%B0%E3%83%AB%E3%83%BC%E3%83%97%E5%8D%81%E4%BA%8C%E6%94%AF%E5%9B%A3%E3%80%8D%EF%BC%881985%E5%B9%B4%EF%BC%89/) (2020-04-25) · Reliability: **Community record** (the author’s interview with tencho)

This is the longest article in the “Column” category. The author states that it is based entirely on a direct interview with tencho, leader of Jūnishidan, and that the linked *Jūnishidan Video* files were copied from the master tapes by tencho himself.

### Group Profile

| Item | Details |
| --- | --- |
| Active | 1985–1989 |
| Organizer | Kikaku Group Jūnishidan |
| Events | Mōketto / Toraketto / Usaketto / Tatsuketto / Jaketto |
| Members | Volunteers from the Kindai University Manga Research Club, about 20–30 people |
| Activities | Organizing dōjin events and related activities |
| Video work | *Jūnishidan Video* |

### The Event Concept

- Around 1985, dōjinshi conventions were becoming mainstream. Small conventions took place in Kansai roughly every month, with attendance ranging from a few hundred to about 1,000.
- Most were uniform affairs: events in name only, amounting to hall rental and sales.
- Jūnishidan wanted a convention that could be enjoyed like a festival. The core remained a dōjinshi exhibition and sale, but buying and selling was not the main purpose; the aim was for exhibiting circles and general attendees alike to have fun. To that end, the group ran planned attractions in a medium-sized hall next to the main hall.

### Name and Venue

- The first event was held in 1985, the Year of the Ox, and named “Ox-Year Comic Market: Mōketto.” The name blends *mō* (the sound of a cow, and a pun on *mōkeru*, “to make a profit”) with *market*.
- Because the event name came from the Chinese zodiac (*jūnishi*), the group called itself Kikaku Group Jūnishidan (“Planning Group Zodiac Corps”).
- Besides the convention, there were cosplay shows, anime-song singing contests, and other attractions. **Screenings of edited parody videos** were one of them.
- The venue was Osaka City Central Public Hall (Nakanoshima Public Hall), a historic building designated an Important Cultural Property of Japan.
- The author describes the event as having an overwhelming degree of freedom for its time, and it was highly rated by attendees.

### The Predecessor: Bisuketto Executive Committee {#bisuketto}

| Item | Details |
| --- | --- |
| Active | 1984 |
| Organizer | Bisuketto Executive Committee |
| Event | Vista Market |
| Members | Kindai University SF Research Club, Manga Research Club, and Anime Research Club (the last as day-of staff only), about 30 people |
| Video work | *Bisuketto Video* |

- Online sources disagree on whether Jūnishidan was founded in 1984 or 1985. The author asked tencho directly and confirmed that Jūnishidan was founded in 1985, but that it had a predecessor called the Bisuketto Executive Committee.
- This committee screened anime opening compilations and other footage in a corner of its dōjin event, Vista Market. Among them was the *Bisuketto Video*.
- The *Bisuketto Video* combined MAD elements with independent-production elements, sitting right at the transition between the two. Besides live-action commercial parodies and news parodies, it already contained what would now be called opening-replacement MAD.
- It was shown without announcement on a small television in a corner of the venue, so only people near the screen could see it, yet the response was enormous.
- The Bisuketto Executive Committee disbanded that year. In 1985, volunteers from the Kindai University Manga Research Club took over the convention and founded Jūnishidan.

### Content and Production Philosophy of the Jūnishidan Videos

- In a corner of its dōjin events, Jūnishidan produced and screened what were then called parody videos.
- According to tencho, many other dōjin videos existed at the time, but the widespread view was that “only Jūnishidan was making weird stuff,” which is why its video came to be seen as the first publicly recognized MAD.
- The author argues that the *Jūnishidan Video* laid almost the entire foundation of video-based MAD: parody, opening-sequence works, music-video works, lyric replacement, commercial parodies, subtitle punchlines, eyecatches, hand-drawn segments, and skits starring the members themselves. On the technical side, it already introduced early forms of CG, on-screen text, and effects. In the author’s view, no MAD has yet appeared that falls outside these broad categories.
- In the 1980s, when entertainment and information were scarce, Jūnishidan adopted the slogan 「本当に面白いものは自分で作るしかない」 (“If you want something truly fun, you have to make it yourself”).
- The production philosophy: one compilation a year, each introducing something new. Holding an audience for 30 minutes requires many devices, but too many makes the work hard to follow and boring. Songs should be as mainstream as possible so viewers can sing along while watching. Lulls and breathing room should be built in deliberately.
- The author says this influenced him more than anything else and is the origin of his habit of changing genre with every work. He calls it his very first textbook, the one that taught him that MAD is entertainment.
- tencho, the person in charge of making the videos, handled planning and production when Jūnishidan was founded, while still a first-year student. He became leader from the second event, stepped down after the third, Usaketto, and remained a key figure afterwards.
- The *Jūnishidan Video* was made for screening at the events and **was never distributed to the general public**, apart from a few insiders.

### The Annual Videos

| Year | Event | Characteristics of the video |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1985 | Mōketto | A music-TV-style structure hosted in DJ format |
| 1986 | Toraketto 86 | A series of subtitle punchlines |
| 1987 | Usaketto 87 | Opening-sequence gags, some subtitle gags |
| 1988 | Tatsuketto 88 | Music videos and drama-style subtitle gags |
| 1989 | Jaketto 89 | Running gags, with dialogue inserted into instrumental breaks |
| 2002 | *Kaettekita Jūnishidan Video* (*The Return of the Jūnishidan Video*) | Transition to nonlinear editing |

How each year unfolded:

- **1985, Mōketto**: The group originally planned to disband after this single event, but it was so successful that a second year was approved.
- **1986, Toraketto**: Held with the same format as the previous year.
- **1987, Usaketto**: Under fire regulations, Nakanoshima Public Hall’s medium hall and small conference rooms together held 700 people, yet more than 1,200 attended that year. The screening room held 200 and showed the video three times, but many attendees still could not see it. After the event, all the founding members graduated, the group disbanded for a time, and tencho stepped down as leader.
- **1988, Tatsuketto**: Junior members wanted to continue, so a successor was appointed. Three months before the event, however, the junior chair and planning lead said they could not prepare in time or produce the video segment. tencho hurriedly returned as leader and reassembled the founding members, and the event went ahead. Attendance hit a record 3,000, more than double the previous year, reportedly because of the huge response to the Usaketto video. Even with three screenings a day, only 600 people could watch, and problems began to occur.
- **The lending problem**: Once the *Jūnishidan Video* caught on, other events flooded the group with requests to borrow it. At first it was lent in good faith, but trouble followed, so the Tatsuketto and Jaketto videos were never distributed or lent outside the group.
- **1989, Jaketto**: Unable to forget the excitement of the previous year, the group held the event once more. By then the staff had all graduated and were busy with work, and Jūnishidan faded away as an event organizer.
- **2002, *The Return of the Jūnishidan Video***: Studio Maruki, which had long been in contact with the group, reached out to tencho. At the Osaka “Jungle” screening event, *The Return of the Jūnishidan Video* marked the group’s debut with nonlinear editing.

### Related Broadcasts

The author’s channel aired two Jūnishidan specials on Niconico Live, on 2019-03-31 and 2019-04-14. The author says the broadcasts covered some topics he could not write about on the blog.

## Comparing the Two Accounts

| Question | 2018 *The Genealogy of MAD* | 2020 revised articles |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Origin of MAD | 1976, *Kichigai Teifu* | 1973, the radio show *Kin-chan no Don to Itte Miyou!*; 1976 as “appearance of audio MAD” |
| Earliest MAD video | 1984, *Etoketto Video* | 1984 *Bisuketto Video* as a transitional work; 1985 *Jūnishidan Video* as the first recognized MAD video |
| Meaning of “MAD” | Author’s earlier reading: “the creator is mad” | Per the *NEW MAD TAPE* notes: “the listener’s memory goes mad” |

| Basis | Author’s own research and consensus in the scene | Direct interview with Jūnishidan leader tencho |

**MAD DOC editorial note**: The *Etoketto Video* series named in the 2018 article appears to correspond to the annual Jūnishidan event names (Mōketto, Toraketto, Usaketto, and so on); *eto* means the Chinese zodiac. It may simply be a collective name for the same videos, but the original never says so explicitly, so this remains unverified. The same blog’s [classification article](./theory#classification) also says Jūnishidan established six genre branches in “1984,” which conflicts with the revised founding year of 1985.
