# Media, Distribution, and Video Platforms

::: tip Key points
- The author argues that “the history of MAD is the history of media”: roughly every ten years a new medium arrives, changing how MAD is made and what the word means.
- Cassette tapes in the 1980s, VHS compilations in the 1990s, DVD in the 2000s, and Niconico and other video sites from 2007.
- In the analog era, footage had to be digitized with VCRs and capture cards; this page records how the author did it.
- The author analyzed Niconico's changes in 2018 and 2020, attributing users' move to YouTube mainly to differing business models.
- Includes livestream audio notes: voice about −5 dB, BGM about −24 dB, video about −15 dB, sound effects about −5 dB.
:::

One of the author’s central arguments is that **the history of MAD is also the history of media**. Each generation of storage and distribution media shaped how MAD was made, how it circulated, and even what the word “MAD” meant. This page summarizes the five “Column” articles that deal with media, platforms, and the publishing environment.

> See the [Column Summaries overview](/en/history/dennouemaki) for the source registration and how reliability is labeled.

## Old Stories of Media and Distribution {#media-history}

Source: [メディアと流通のむかし話](https://dennouemaki.blog.jp/2018/04/21/%E3%83%A1%E3%83%87%E3%82%A3%E3%82%A2%E3%81%A8%E6%B5%81%E9%80%9A%E3%81%AE%E3%82%80%E3%81%8B%E3%81%97%E8%A9%B1/) (2018-04-21) · Reliability: **Community record** (first-hand recollection) / **Personal view** (views on the future)

The author organizes each era by its main media, editing method, and distribution channels.

### 1980s: The MAD Tape Era

- **Main medium**: Cassette tape.
- **Editing method**: The prototype of the MAD tape is said to date back to the 1970s. Creators used the dubbing function of double-cassette decks to splice various sounds together.
- **Distribution**: Reportedly circulated through university clubs and events such as Comic Market.

### 1990s: The Analog MAD Video Era

- **Main media**: VHS and S-VHS tape.
- **Editing method**: Two VCRs were used to string footage together by dubbing from one to the other. The author marvels that this demanded holding the entire finished piece in one’s head, calling it “divine skill.”
- **Equipment**: VHS matured in the late 1990s, and many consumer VCRs with near-professional specifications appeared. Some creators bought dedicated title generators (*telopper*) or even full editing controllers, which fueled a rumor that MAD makers were industry insiders. (The author adds that some actually were.)
- **Typical decks**: Popular among consumers were the HR-X5 (list price ¥225,000) and HR-X7 (list price ¥250,000), released by Victor (the former Victor Company of Japan) for professional use. Linking two or three allowed editing equivalent to a professional setup. The author used a second-hand X5 to dub distribution copies and recalls that the S-video cable alone cost about ¥10,000.
- **Distribution**: Reportedly handed out within small communities such as dōjin events and screening events.
- **The internet and the post**: In the late 1990s the internet spread and grassroots networks flourished nationwide; this is when the author got involved. Creators began arranging trades by email and mailing tapes to each other. The author jokes that videotapes arriving in the mail looked highly suspicious, and that his own parents grew wary.
- **Spread**: Falling VCR prices accelerated things further. Copies begat copies, and MAD’s reputation spread rapidly among hardcore anime fans, an era the author likens to the Cambrian explosion.

### 1998: The Hybrid MAD Video Era

- **Main media**: VHS, S-VHS, data CD, and Video CD.
- **Editing method**: Analog (VHS) → digital (PC) → analog (VHS); only the editing stage was nonlinear.
- **Context**: Internet connections were still slow, so VHS remained dominant. After Hajiou’s *Otaku no MAD*, however, data versions stored on CD began to increase.
- **Turning point**: The author regards *Otaku no MAD Video* (1997–98) as the spark of the digital editing era, ushering in what he calls the “Warring States period” of MAD.

### 2000s: The Digital MAD Video Era

- **Main media**: DVD-Video and data DVD.
- **Editing method**: With the spread of DVD, a fully digital DVD → PC → DVD workflow arrived.
- **Distribution**: In the early 2000s, file-sharing software such as WinMX and Winny appeared and spread MAD videos across the internet. Copies begat copies, and the name MAD became known beyond anime fandom.
- **Underground character**: The author recalls MAD videos taking on an even stronger underground flavor and often appearing in hacking-oriented magazines.
- **DVD-R**: The author recalls that Pioneer released Japan’s first domestic DVD-R drive around 2001 (he remembers a price of about ¥100,000). Other makers followed, prices fell, and DVD-Video became the popular format in offline communities.
- **The author’s own role**: The author claims to have been the person who first distributed DVDs across Japan, in 2002. He adds that the first to successfully produce a DVD was “C,” and the first to hand them out to acquaintances was “M.” Only foreign DVD authoring software existed, and the medium was buggy. He describes burning 200 discs under grueling conditions: ¥1,000 per DVD-R, 1x write speed, and a failure rate of about 10%.

### 2010s: The MAD Movie Era

- **Main media**: Blu-ray Disc and video services such as Niconico Douga, YouTube, and the now-defunct ZOOME.
- **Distribution**: Offline, Blu-ray gradually came into use, but compiling works onto a single disc survived only in some screening-centered communities. Most users moved to video-sharing sites.
- **Change in terminology**: In the author’s view, the original sense of “MAD video” (a compilation) was lost, and the umbrella term shifted to “MAD movie,” meaning a work watched on its own.

### 2020s: The MAD ??? Era

- The author suggests that, judging by past patterns, another turning point is due. MAD’s forms are tied to media life cycles, and the scene is waiting for the next medium.
- He also worries that, with YouTube tightening copyright enforcement in July 2018, Niconico users steadily leaving for YouTube, and no clear trigger to bring viewers and followers back, MAD creators may be losing their sense of purpose.
- He concludes that, online or offline, the means of communication hold the key, and ends by wondering whether the future lies in 4K or in some medium yet unseen.

The article cites the Japanese Wikipedia entry “MADムービー” and the Niconico Pedia entry “MADテープ” as references.

## Video Capture in the Analog Era {#capture}

Source: [ビデオキャプチャという作業（アナログ時代）](https://dennouemaki.blog.jp/2018/12/11/%E3%83%93%E3%83%87%E3%82%AA%E3%82%AD%E3%83%A3%E3%83%97%E3%83%81%E3%83%A3%E3%81%A8%E3%81%84%E3%81%86%E4%BD%9C%E6%A5%AD%EF%BC%88%E3%82%A2%E3%83%8A%E3%83%AD%E3%82%B0%E6%99%82%E4%BB%A3%EF%BC%89/) (2018-12-11) · Reliability: **Community record** (first-hand recollection)

- From the arrival of Windows in 1995 until around 2005, VHS was the dominant video medium, and the standard method was analog capture from tape to PC.
- Hard drives held around 10 GB, which the author puts at roughly one ten-thousandth of today’s capacity. Creators had to hunt through piles of tapes for the scenes they wanted and capture them second by second, which took great patience.
- Because this step largely determined the cut structure, the author says it is no exaggeration to call it half of all the work.
- The golden rule was not to touch the computer during capture, to avoid dropped frames.
- The author mentions that, while recently transferring old DV tapes to a PC, old habits still made him afraid to touch the machine.

## Niconico Douga as a Medium (2018) {#niconico-2018}

Source: [ニコニコ動画というメディアの話](https://dennouemaki.blog.jp/2018/05/19/%E3%83%8B%E3%82%B3%E3%83%8B%E3%82%B3%E3%81%AE%E8%A9%B1/) (2018-05-19) · Reliability: **Personal view** (personal opinion responding to news coverage at the time)

- **Context**: On 2018-05-19, several outlets reported on Niconico Douga’s earnings outlook. The figures were broadly as expected, and the author imagines users had mixed feelings.
- **Voices from the scene**: The author relays a comment from a MAD editor active on Niconico: the site is getting emptier, and MAD itself is losing popularity. The author takes this as a sign that users themselves feel the exodus.
- **Where users went**: Many are said to have moved to YouTube, and some reportedly to free Chinese video sites.
- **Causes**: The author attributes the exodus to problems with user fees and creator compensation. For MAD, revenue is beside the point, but when viewers decline, moving to a larger platform is unavoidable.
- **Media life cycle**: About 12 years after its launch, Niconico could plausibly be entering the decline phase of a media life cycle. The author asks whether it will offer a new means of communication or whether a new medium will appear.
- **On YouTube**: The author describes YouTube as a system that distills the capitalism of the Google empire, which is why it grew so large, but personally feels it does not suit Japanese otaku culture.
- **The essence of MAD**: The author sees MAD as a tool for communication between users; **talking with others about the anime you love** is, to him, the essence of MAD. From 2ch to Niconico, shared feeling and empathy give rise to homage, and quietly building a solid community is the root of otaku culture.

## MAD Media and the Future of Niconico Douga (2020) {#niconico-2020}

Source: [MADのメディアとニコニコ動画のみらい](https://dennouemaki.blog.jp/2020/08/01/mad%E3%81%AE%E3%83%A1%E3%83%87%E3%82%A3%E3%82%A2%E3%81%A8%E3%83%8B%E3%82%B3%E3%83%8B%E3%82%B3%E5%8B%95%E7%94%BB%E3%81%AE%E3%81%BF%E3%82%89%E3%81%84/) (2020-08-01) · Reliability: **Personal view** (personal opinion and business commentary; corporate results and personnel details should be checked against official announcements)

### 1. A Ten-Year Cycle in MAD Media

- The author restates that MAD history is media history and observes that each generation of media takes roughly ten years to reach its peak.
- He suggests this is because rising video resolution, and the next-generation media that accompany it, arrive on roughly a ten-year cycle.

### 2. Niconico Membership

- Niconico Douga began in 2006 (the author gives 2006 here; the 2018 genealogy article gives 2007 as the service launch). From 2017, premium membership began to decline, curiously just after the tenth anniversary.
- The author attributes this mainly to users moving to YouTube, which offers higher image quality, free uploading and viewing, and even ad revenue. (Niconico premium members can also watch in high quality.)
- He sees the outcome as a consequence of differing business models.

### 3. DWANGO’s Restructuring

- In the author’s view, DWANGO briefly became profitable, but its over-extended businesses became a burden, and after 2017 falling users were compounded by growing losses.
- He writes that KADOKAWA announced a major restructuring in 2019. Niconico Douga and Niconico Chōkaigi were positioned as loss-making divisions with a market-development role, while Niconico Channel, games, events, and other businesses covered the operating costs in a diversified model.
- The author summarizes former president Nobuo Kawakami’s philosophy as “intellectual property should be shared and developed,” that is, a desire to promote the dōjin market, and sees Niconico Douga as the product of that philosophy.
- He records that Kawakami stepped down in fiscal 2019 because of poor results and that Takeshi Natsuno of KADOKAWA became representative director. He describes Kawakami as a user-oriented startup president and Natsuno as a seasoned executive: KADOKAWA senior managing director, chairman of Movie Walker, and in his youth a developer of i-mode.
- He states that after the sweeping restructuring, DWANGO turned from a loss to a tripling of earnings within a year (his wording; no source for the figures is given).

### 4. DWANGO’s Vision

- The author sees Kawakami’s idea as follows: even a niche gathering place for socially awkward people can grow a new subculture, given the right communication tools.
- He cites creators and artists who emerged from Niconico: Hatsune Miku-related creators, Kenshi Yonezu, ClariS, HoneyWorks, Eir Aoi, and others.
- In his view, because developing the market was itself the goal, the means of monetizing it were weak.
- He quotes Matsuo Bashō’s principle of *fueki ryūkō*: remain true to what is unchanging while embracing what is new, since the constant pursuit of novelty is itself the unchanging essence.

### 5. Outlook for Niconico

- The author believes Niconico Channel was the main source of revenue in 2020; that the live venue Hare Studio was in serious difficulty under the pandemic; and that holding Chōkaigi online that year might actually save around ¥100 million (his estimate).
- He argues that Niconico Douga is worth keeping even at a loss, since it has led Japanese subculture and pioneered the online video market, and that it deserves more government support.
- With Japanese subculture now global, he sees overseas support as the top priority. KADOKAWA is working on this, so Niconico’s business model may change.
- He criticizes Niconico’s interface as hard to use and suggests simplifying it and adding more English to make it accessible to overseas users.

## Behind the Scenes of a Niconico Livestream: Audio Notes {#livestream-audio}

Source: [ニコ生配信のウラかた（音声環境備忘録）](https://dennouemaki.blog.jp/2020/04/25/%E3%83%8B%E3%82%B3%E7%94%9F%E9%85%8D%E4%BF%A1%E3%81%AE%E3%82%A6%E3%83%A9%E3%81%8B%E3%81%9F%EF%BC%88%E9%9F%B3%E5%A3%B0%E7%92%B0%E5%A2%83%E5%82%99%E5%BF%98%E9%8C%B2%EF%BC%89/) (2020-04-25) · Reliability: **Personal view** (personal experience; software and service details as of 2019–2020)

The MAD-themed Niconico livestreams the author takes part in (such as the [Jūnishidan specials](./origins#junishidan)) involve several members on a voice call. Describing himself as quiet-voiced and not very articulate, the author experimented repeatedly with equipment and voice services and recorded the following.

### Problems Encountered

- At first there was microphone white noise, equipment noise, insufficient gain, echo, feedback, voices too loud or too quiet, and even arguments between members.
- Once the hardware was mostly sorted, he found that free voice chat runs at only about 30 kbps, enough for speech alone. (For comparison, he cites about 32 kbps for AM radio and 128 kbps or more for MP4 audio.) The limited frequency band also caused music to break up on the other end.

### Livestream Preparation Checklist

**1. Environment**

- Watch out for ambient noise: air conditioners, sound from neighboring rooms, clothing and cables brushing.
- Keep the livestream’s own audio from being picked up again by the microphone, to prevent echo.
- Use blankets or acoustic material for basic soundproofing.

**2. Microphone**

- For indoor recording, use a condenser microphone (high sensitivity, suited to fixed placement); dynamic microphones resist noise and suit handheld use.
- Prefer a directional microphone: ambient noise does not disappear, but the voice becomes clearer.
- Use a pop filter or windscreen.
- Use a USB audio interface, and avoid cheap USB cables.
- If white noise is severe, a microphone preamp can solve it.
- Turn off the microphone’s built-in noise reduction and lower its input sensitivity; too much makes it harder to listen to.
- Do not use Bluetooth microphones or call from a smartphone; both drop out on unstable connections, and phones also cause echo.
- Wear headphones to prevent echo.
- Unplug mobile devices from mains power to avoid hum.
- In the author’s experience, microphone quality scales with price; cheap ones are noisy.

**3. PC Settings**

- Turn off Windows automatic volume adjustment, microphone boost, and “Listen to this device.”
- Do not overdo noise suppression in the streaming software.
- Balance the voice with compression and gain. Pay more attention to the gain on the streaming side than to input gain.
- Reference levels the author uses: voice −5 dB, BGM −24 dB, video/music −15 dB, sound effects −5 dB.
- An auto-gain plugin makes things much easier.

**4. Voice Chat Services**

- In the author’s view, free calling services such as Skype mostly deliver AM-radio quality (about 32 kbps) and cut frequencies outside the human voice range, so audio may sound muffled, distorted, or choppy on poor connections. Paid web-conferencing services, with guaranteed bandwidth and wider frequency range, sound somewhat better.
- Discord’s server voice channels, introduced in 2019, allow 64 kbps or more (screen sharing was not available at the time), and paying raises this to 128–256 kbps.
- His personal ratings of free calling: LINE ×, Hangouts △, Skype ◎, Discord ◎, Discord server voice ◎, SkyPhone ◎ (not tried himself; reportedly the best quality, but mobile-only at the time).

### Streaming and Support Software

- For streaming software, the choice was between N Air, DWANGO’s free OBS-based app, and OBS itself. N Air’s interface is almost identical to OBS, the main difference being automatic connection to Niconico Live. The author found N Air still buggy and used OBS.
- For member voice chat, he used Skype. He had tried LINE, Hangouts, and others, but settled on Skype for stability, and was then testing Discord, which reportedly allows higher bitrates than Skype (about 64 kbps) with a technical trick and per-person volume control.
- Because Niconico Live’s delay varies, members often talked past each other. The author built a setup that shares the streaming software’s screen with others during the call while feeding the BGM back through a virtual mixer: Skype + OBS + VoiceMeeter. He admits it was extremely difficult to put together.
- Because some comments failed to appear on Niconico Live, he used the comment viewer “Yarimasu! Anko-chan,” noting that it reveals the usernames of viewers who have turned off anonymity (the “184” setting), although most viewers stay anonymous, so he still does not know who is watching.
