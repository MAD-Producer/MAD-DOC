# MAD·AMV Historical Timeline

::: tip Key points
- MAD·AMV has three parallel origins: **Japanese MAD**, **Western AMV**, and the **Chinese community**.
- **Japan**: audio collage tapes in Osaka in the late 1970s → Jūnishidan's parody edit videos in 1985 → computer editing around 1998 → Niconico Douga in 2007.
- **West**: Jim Kaposztas edited the earliest documented AMV with two VCRs in 1982 → convention contests → AnimeMusicVideos.org around 2000.
- **China**: MAD Tieba founded in 2004 → Bilibili's MAD contests drove growth from 2013 → in 2019 many old Tieba posts became inaccessible and the community spread to Bilibili and other platforms.
- Much early history rests on participants' memories, so every event is labeled for reliability.
:::

## Japanese MAD {#japanese-mad}

### 1973–1978: Audio Collage Tapes <Badge type="warning" text="Community record / Unverified" />

- Jūnishidan leader tencho recalls that the source goes back to the radio show *Kin-chan no Don to Itte Miyō!* around 1973, which had a segment splicing record lyrics based on listener ideas.
- In 1976, MAD島川 and ネトホヘル矢野 (the manga artist Kentarō Yano) at Osaka University of Arts made the audio collage tape “奇知害低符,” considered the earliest audio MAD.
- In 1978, *NEW MAD TAPE*, re-edited by クレイジィ今井, began to circulate. According to tencho, “MAD” comes from the tape's liner notes: people who heard it found the songs in their heads scrambled and could no longer sing them the original way.

These accounts rest mainly on participants' memories and retellings; the original tapes and contemporaneous texts still need checking. See [Dennou Emaki: Origins, Etymology, and Jūnishidan](/en/history/dennouemaki/origins).

### 1984–1989: Parody Edit Videos <Badge type="info" text="Community record" />

- In 1984, a Kindai University student group screened “びすけっとビデオ” in a corner of a fan event; it already included “OP replacement” and is a transitional work.
- In 1985, members of Kindai University's manga club formed “Kikaku Group Jūnishidan,” ran fan events at Osaka's Nakanoshima Public Hall, and screened a “Jūnishidan Video” every year (1985–1989). It is regarded as the first MAD video recognized by the public, then called a “parody edit video.”
- Parody, OP replacement, music videos, replacement lyrics, ad parodies, and the other basic forms of anime MAD were all present by then.
- Around 1990, a creator called “スタジオまるき” released a tape titled “MAD VTR,” which may be how the term “MAD video” spread.

### 1990s: Videotape and Computer Editing <Badge type="info" text="Community record" />

- In the early 1990s, MAD circulated as VHS compilations at fan events and screenings.
- Some creators began non-linear editing on the Mac around 1996–1997; in 1998 Windows 98 and affordable capture cards spread, the number of MAD creators rose quickly, and P2P file sharing spread works widely.

For details of the media shift, see [Dennou Emaki: Media, Distribution, and Video Platforms](/en/history/dennouemaki/media).

### Late 1990s–2000s: Still-Image M@D and the Web <Badge type="info" text="Community record" />

Visual-novel and game CG, manga, and illustrations were separated into layers, animated, and set to music, forming the still-image M@D tradition. Personal websites, download sites, and later video sites changed how works spread.

### 2007 Onward: Niconico Douga <Badge type="info" text="Community record" />

After Niconico Douga launched, production went fully digital and many young creators joined. The culture of “MAD videos” as tape compilations faded, and the term shifted to “MAD movie” for individual works. Activity split into online (Niconico, YouTube) and offline screenings. From the late 2010s, editing moved further onto mobile devices.

## Western AMV {#western-amv}

### 1982: The Earliest Documented AMV <Badge type="tip" text="Verifiable" />

Research by scholar Ian Roberts and AMV community histories treat Jim Kaposztas's work as one of the earliest known Western fan AMVs: using two VCRs, he cut footage from *Space Battleship Yamato / Star Blazers* to a Beatles song.

### Late 1980s–1990s: Tapes and Conventions <Badge type="tip" text="Verifiable" />

The circulation of anime tapes and fansubs gave creators access to footage, and AMVs spread through convention screenings and tape compilations. Convention contests gradually settled on categories such as Action, Comedy, and Drama. AMV Japan lists the 1992 Anime Expo contest as an early milestone.

### Late 1990s: Digital Editing <Badge type="tip" text="Verifiable" />

Capture cards, larger hard drives, and non-linear editing software made frame-level control, precise sync, masking, and compositing widespread. Roberts sees 1990–2001 as the key period in which the digital AMV scene took shape.

### Around 2000: Online Community <Badge type="info" text="Verifiable / Community record" />

AnimeMusicVideos.org was founded and gradually took on the roles of a works database, creator profiles, forums, downloads, and community norms. International contests and specialist sites then developed their own categories and judging cultures. Works such as *Odorikuruu*, *Euphoria*, and *AMV Hell* in the 2000s pushed dance sync, complex compositing, and comedy compilations forward.

## Chinese Community {#chinese}

### 2004–2007: Forums and Early MAD Tieba <Badge type="info" text="Community record" />

The Baidu MAD Tieba page shows a founding date of **2004-12-06** (some older records say December 7). Early creators were active on forums, personal sites, download sites, and fansub communities; many of those links are now dead.

### 2008–2012: Community Building and Newcomer Growth <Badge type="info" text="Community record" />

The “MAD Tieba Chronicle, 2008–2019” treats 2008 as the start of systematic community building: moderator changes, resource consolidation, a video library, tutorial collections, and events such as MADeMAD. From 2009 to 2012 there were tournaments, newcomer contests, and festival collaborations, and tutorials and MEPs multiplied. Many tutorials depended on cloud drives, Tieba attachments, and hotlinked images, and service shutdowns caused the first wave of losses.

### 2013–2016: Video Platforms and Expansion <Badge type="info" text="Community record" />

Bilibili's 2013 MAD contest brought wide exposure, newcomers, and debates about commercialization; 2014 saw a concentrated rise in creators and events. In 2015–2016, groups such as DrawMAD appeared and exchange with overseas styles grew, while disputes over copyright detection, templated work, and re-edits became more visible.

### 2017–2019: Turmoil on Tieba and a Break in the Record <Badge type="info" text="Community record" />

MAD Tieba went through moderator changes and community conflict in 2017–2018 before recovering. In 2019, Tieba's content moderation made a large number of posts from before 2017 temporarily or permanently inaccessible, severely affecting years of tutorials and discussion. Many links in the newcomer navigation post are still deleted or missing images today.

### 2020s: Dispersal and Continuity <Badge type="info" text="Verifiable / Community record" />

Creator activity spread across Bilibili profiles, articles, contest accounts, chat groups, and overseas platforms, and split into a Bilibili long-form community and a style-focused short-edit community (see [Types and Classification](/en/mad/classification)). Wuyi无疑's 2021 review, Bilibili's 2022 MAD Creation Festival, and the 2024 MAD contest show that public activity continues. Tieba's decline does not mean Chinese MAD·AMV has disappeared.
